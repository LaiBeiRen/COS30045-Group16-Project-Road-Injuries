The Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics publishes road-crash hospitalisation series based on AIHW hospital data. This project uses Group 16’s prepared CSV extracts for 2011–2021.

Visit the original dataset

Each chart keeps the coverage of its own extract. Filters act within that chart.

What do these measures mean?

Hospitalisations are the counts in the supplied extracts, not a count of unique people. Bed days are the supplied total patient days. Bed days per hospitalisation is total bed days divided by total hospitalisations for the same selection.

This aggregate ratio is not a patient-level length-of-stay estimate. Bed days are reproduced as supplied; their mortality adjustment needs confirmation against the team’s workflow.

Can every year be compared directly?

The BITRE dictionary identifies admission-policy changes in Victoria in 2012 and NSW in 2017. A rehabilitation-coding change also affected bed-day counting from 2015–16. These changes can affect trends independently of road crashes.

A change around 2020 is an observation in these data. The chart alone cannot establish its cause.

Read the data dictionary
Why do the chart totals differ?

The road-user file contains 410,884 hospitalisations. The age/sex file contains 410,868, which is 16 fewer. The three-area remoteness file contains 402,051, which is 8,833 fewer.

The supplied files cover different sets of categories. The demographic file has no year field, and the remoteness file contains only Major Cities, Regional and Remote. The website preserves these exports and does not combine them into a single cross-filtered dataset. The precise exclusions should be reconciled in the KNIME workflow.

What can counts tell us about risk?

These extracts have no population or travel-exposure denominators. Higher counts do not establish higher individual risk. Different age-band widths and group sizes also affect comparisons. Bed-day burden can reflect care and reporting differences as well as injuries.
